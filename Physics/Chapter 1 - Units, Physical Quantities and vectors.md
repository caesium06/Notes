# Learning Goals

By studying this chapter, you will
learn:

* Three fundamental quantities of
physics and the units physicists 
use to measure them.
* How to keep track of significant 
figures in your calculations.
* The difference between scalars and
vectors, and how to add and sub
tract vectors graphically.
* What the components of a vector
are, and how to use them in 
calculations.
* What unit vectors are, and how 
to use them with components to
describe vectors.
* Two ways of multiplying vectors


### 1. IDENTIFY

First determine **what physics is involved**.

Ask:

* What physical situation is described?
* Which **concepts/laws** apply?
* What are the **target variables** — what am I being asked to find?
* What **known quantities** are given or implied?
* What assumptions or **idealized models** are appropriate?
* Roughly what answer should I expect?

For example, if an object is thrown upward, you might identify:

* Concept: **kinematics**
* Known: initial velocity, gravitational acceleration
* Target: maximum height
* Model: constant gravitational acceleration, negligible air resistance

### 2. SET UP

Translate the physical situation into mathematics.

* Choose the relevant equations.
* Match the variables in the problem to the variables in those equations.
* Decide which equations are actually needed.
* Draw a diagram/sketch if it helps.
* Establish signs and coordinate directions where necessary.

The important idea is: **don't start calculating until you know which equations describe the situation.**

### 3. EXECUTE

Now perform the mathematical solution.

This can involve:

* Algebraic manipulation
* Substitution
* Numerical calculation
* Unit conversion

Keep the algebra organized before plugging in numbers whenever possible.

### 4. EVALUATE

Check whether the answer makes physical sense.

Ask:

* Does it agree with my initial estimate?
* Are the **units** correct?
* Is the magnitude reasonable?
* Does the sign make sense?
* What happens in limiting cases?
* Does the result agree with the expected physical behavior?

For example, if you calculate that a dropped object reaches the ground with a speed of `10,000 m/s` after falling only 2 m, you should immediately recognize that something went wrong.

---

### The complete framework

**IDENTIFY → SET UP → EXECUTE → EVALUATE**

A useful way to remember it is:

> **What is happening? → How do I model it? → Do the math → Does the answer make sense?**

This is essentially a **general-purpose method for solving physics problems**, and it's much more valuable than memorizing individual formulas. The biggest skill is learning to move from the **physical description** of a problem to the **appropriate mathematical model**.
